# Social Post Playbook

## Universal Anatomy

1. Hook: first line must earn the next line.
2. Story move: one proof-led product story, origin story, audience story, tiny scene, surprise, opinion, community prompt, collector observation, gift moment, or maker detail.
3. Optional proof: sales data, review themes, customer behavior, community signal, or a product detail only when it makes the story stronger.
4. CTA: one clear action matched to the goal.

Use line breaks for scanability. Never produce a dense paragraph for Facebook or Instagram unless the user asks for a long-form caption.

Do not default to `hook -> product description -> CTA`. That structure is usually too salesy for MattMadeMe social content. Product facts are optional support, not the main attraction.

## Social Middle Rule

The middle must stay social. It should continue the chosen story move with personality, a tiny scene, a playful opinion, audience meaning, or community tension before product details appear.

Use product details only when they earn their place as:

- a punchline;
- proof of the personality;
- a detail that sharpens the scene;
- a reason to gift, collect, hide, name, or share the duck;
- a genuinely interesting detail that would catch the right buyer's attention.

Leave product details out when they feel forced, generic, obvious from the image, or likely to break the rhythm.

Do not use the middle as a catalog sentence:

- no stacked feature lists such as "with a helmet, vest, grey accents, and...";
- no all-purpose use-case piles such as "great for desks, gifts, cruise hiding, and shelves";
- no product-category filler such as "this 3D printed collectible brings..." unless the platform is Pinterest or the user asked for product-forward copy.

Before finalizing, ask: could the middle sentence be pasted into an Etsy listing with little or no editing? If yes, rewrite it with more personality, scene, attitude, or community meaning.

## Story Moves

Choose one move before writing:

- Tiny scene: place the duck in a specific moment, shelf, desk, cruise cabin, gift exchange, or tiny job.
- Proof-led product story: explain what happened around the product, who responded, and why it matters.
- Origin story: explain why the product exists, what sparked it, or what customer/audience need it answers.
- Audience story: make the role, hobby, relationship, or community the emotional center.
- Surprise detail: make one unexpected detail the reason to keep reading.
- Playful opinion: give the audience a small claim to react to.
- Community prompt: ask where it should be hidden, named, gifted, collected, or sent next.
- Collector observation: speak to flock-building, sets, tiny themes, or the joy of oddly specific ducks.
- Gift moment: start with the recipient or occasion, then let the product fit that moment.
- Maker detail: use a real process/craft detail only when supplied.

## Proof-Led Product Story

Use this route when there is real evidence of traction or audience meaning: order count, repeated requests, recent sales, comments, reviews, customer group, gift occasion, personalization trend, or a surprising use case.

This route is especially useful for avoiding the sea of sameness because it makes the post about what the product means in the world, not what the product is.

Strong prompts to answer before writing:

- What happened?
- Who is responding to it?
- Why this product, why now?
- What human role, relationship, inside joke, appreciation moment, or identity does it serve?
- Which proof point makes this story feel real: sales, reviews, buyer group, gifting pattern, personalization choice, customer photo, social comment, or a truly distinctive product detail?

Example shape:

```text
Mail Duck did not take off because people needed another desk collectible.

It took off because people wanted a small way to say, "I see the person who keeps everything moving."

[MATT_TO_CONFIRM: order count or demand signal] orders have gone to people gifting mail carriers, postal coworkers, and the dependable route regulars who deserve more appreciation than they usually get.

The real story is the thank-you. The customizable hat only matters if it makes that thank-you feel more personal.
```

Do not use "took off," "went wild," "customers love," or order counts unless source facts support them.

Proof hierarchy:

1. Real customer/review language or a clear review theme.
2. Sales or order signals by product, time window, or buyer group.
3. Repeated requests, personalization patterns, or customer use cases.
4. Distinctive product details that create an emotional or social reason to care.
5. Generic product details only for Pinterest/search or when the user asks for product-forward copy.

## Facebook

Best for community, conversation, product personality, and small stories.

- Hook should work in roughly 120 characters for mobile truncation.
- Use a conversational tone that sounds like a person, not a shop listing.
- A direct question often outperforms a statement.
- The middle should create a reply-worthy moment before describing features.
- For products with proof of demand, lead with what happened and who it matters to.
- Links can appear in the body, but only when traffic is the goal.
- Use 0-2 hashtags. Prefer none unless there is a branded/community reason.

Good CTA types:

- "What would you name this one?"
- "Who needs this on their desk?"
- "Which duck should join the flock next?"
- "Find your favorite duck in our Etsy shop."

## Instagram

Best for visuals, saves, shares, and personality.

- First 125 characters carry the caption hook.
- Caption should add meaning to the visual, not describe what is already obvious.
- Give the image a tiny story, inner monologue, or collector payoff.
- When a product has traction, turn the caption into a save/share-worthy mini story about the audience or occasion.
- Use save/share CTAs for gift lists, collections, or ideas.
- Use 3-8 relevant hashtags only when requested or useful.
- Provide alt text when useful.

## Pinterest

Best for searchable gift ideas and product discovery.

- Write a title and description, not a social caption.
- Lead with keywords a buyer would search.
- No hashtag reliance.
- Mention audience, occasion, and product category naturally.
- Keep the description useful for search, but add a save-worthy idea or gift context instead of only listing features.

## Threads

Best for casual personality and community.

- Keep it human and compact.
- Avoid polished ad language.
- Use relatable observation, playful confession, or direct question.

## LinkedIn

Use only when the angle is maker/business/process-focused.

- Lead with a real maker/business insight, not a product pitch.
- Use line breaks every 1-2 sentences.
- CTA should invite discussion, not immediate shopping.

## Cross-Platform Rule

Do not paste the same post across platforms without adapting:

- Facebook gets conversation.
- Instagram gets visual payoff and save/share behavior.
- Pinterest gets search.
- Threads gets casual immediacy.
- LinkedIn gets process insight.

## Scheduling Strategy

Use owned analytics first when they are available. For Meta and Instagram, check audience active-time tools before overriding a planned schedule. For Pinterest, treat timing as part of an evergreen discovery system rather than a short feed spike.

Default MattMadeMe starting points when no account analytics are available:

- Facebook: `18:30` local time. Use for community prompts, collection questions, and post-work browsing.
- Instagram: `12:30` local time. Use for feed captions and visual product moments; test evening alternates such as `19:30` for launches or Reels-style posts.
- Pinterest: `20:30` local time. Use for searchable gift/occasion pins when people are planning, saving, and browsing ideas.
- Email: `09:30` local time. Use a morning window unless the offer has a specific deadline.
- Blog, Etsy, and Website updates: `10:00` local time. These are durable content updates, so clarity and consistency matter more than a narrow social feed window.

Treat these as scheduling hypotheses. Rotate day/time tests by platform, watch reach, saves, clicks, comments, and Etsy visits, then update the default only when MattMadeMe data supports it.
